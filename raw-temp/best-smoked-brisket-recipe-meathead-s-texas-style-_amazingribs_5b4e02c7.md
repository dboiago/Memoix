## Recipe Overview: Smoked Brisket Success Formula

-   **Buy** a 12- to 15-pound [whole packer brisket](https://amazingribs.com/tested-recipes/beef-and-bison-recipes/beef-bison-cuts-not-all-are-created-equal/), [USDA Choice](https://amazingribs.com/tested-recipes/beef-and-bison-recipes/beef-grades-and-labels/) or better. 
-   **Trim** [the fat cap](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/melting-fat-cap-myth/) to about 1/4 inch. 
-   **[Dry brine](https://amazingribs.com/bbq-technique-and-science/wet-brining-vs-dry-brining/)** with approximately 1/2 teaspoon Morton Coarse Kosher Salt per pound of meat. 
-   **Rub** with [Big Bad Beef Rub](https://amazingribs.com/tested-recipes/spice-rubs-and-pastes/big-bad-beef-rub-recipe/), or if you’re a traditionalist, just salt and pepper. 
-   **Smoke** at 225°F with hardwood smoke. Your [choice of smoking wood is not critical](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/grill-and-smoker-setup-and-firing/science-of-wood-and-smoke/).
-   **[Wrap the brisket](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/texas-crutch/)** when the bark is set and the meat hits [the BBQ stall](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/understanding-and-beating-barbecue-stall/), at an internal temp of 150°F to 165°F (after about 6 to 8 hours), and then continue cooking at 225°F.
-   **Hold** when the meat reaches an internal temp of 195°F to 205°F (the jiggly “wubba wubba” stage, after another 5 to 7 hours), by [placing the wrapped brisket in a cooler](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/faux-cambro/) for 2 to 4 hours. 
-   **Slice** when the meat temp drops back down to 150°F, unwrapping the beast, and slicing it across the grain of each muscle.

## The science behind our BBQ brisket recipe

-   **Juicy, tender meat**. Cooking at a low and slow 225°F allows time for [tough connective tissue](https://amazingribs.com/technique-and-science/cooking-science/basic-meat-science/) to soften and become tender. An optional [injection of beef broth](https://amazingribs.com/ratings-reviews/tools/injectors/) keeps the meat extra juicy.
-   **Smoky bark**. [Cooking with hardwood](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/grill-and-smoker-setup-and-firing/science-of-wood-and-smoke/) flavors the surface of the meat with smoke, while evaporation and a BBQ dry rub enhance the bark’s flavor and crunchiness.
-   **Salt for flavor and juiciness**. Salting the meat and leaving it on overnight gives the salt time to penetrate. The other spices in your BBQ rub won’t penetrate, but you want that NaCl flavor amplifier down in the meat where it can also help the proteins retain moisture, the basic result of [brining](file:///tested-recipes/salting-brining-curing-and-injecting/dry-brining/).
-   **Wrapping to beat the stall**. [The Texas Crutch](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/texas-crutch/) is optional but I always do it for brisket. Wrapping the meat powers through [the dreaded BBQ stall](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/understanding-and-beating-barbecue-stall/), minimizes [evaporative cooling](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/understanding-and-beating-barbecue-stall/), cuts cooking time, and retains juices.
-   **Holding the meat to tenderize it**. After cooking, [holding the wrapped meat](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/faux-cambro/) for 1 to 4 hours lets [carryover cooking](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/carryover-cooking/) tenderize the meat further by converting more connective tissue collagen into gelatin.
-   **Slicing for increased tenderness**. When done, slicing the two main brisket muscles against the grain of the muscle makes the meat even easier to chew (a.k.a. more tender). Separate the muscles and you can slice each one properly. Most pitmasters slice their brisket to the thickness of a pencil. I like to pour any retained juices over the slices.

## How to buy the best brisket

![raw whole packer brisket shown fat side up on a white cutting board with a knife.](https://amazingribs.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/12/raw-brisket.jpg)

**Get a good grade.** Go for the highest grade you can find, and hand pick the slab with the most fat striation visible. Pay no attention to the fat cap. You will trim most of that away. But marbling is important. Go for USDA Choice, USDA Prime, or Certified Angus Beef. If you insist on the best and have the cash, buy [Wagyu brisket](https://amazingribs.com/tested-recipes/beef-and-bison-recipes/beef-grades-and-labels/).  Good fat marbling is half the battle when the goal is a tender brisket. And if it is not labeled, chances are it is USDA Select. Avoid it. You will have a hard time elevating it above shoe leather. Please don’t write to me saying you can’t figure out why your brisket was tough if you did not buy USDA Choice or better. If you have to special order the brisket you want, then order it.

**Buy a whole packer.** Go for a whole packer brisket with the point and flat muscles intact. It’ll weigh anywhere from 8 to 16 pounds, depending on the size of the cow (larger specimens tend to be tougher). Some butchers sell only brisket flats (a.k.a. “first cut”) and some may offer the point (a.k.a. “second cut” or “deckle”). Small pieces, like a four pound hunk o’ flat or hunk o’ point lose a lot of moisture, shrink a lot, and get tough. But if that’s your only option, go for the point. Worse case, if they only have flats, buy the thickest one you can. Plan on 1/2 pound or more per person.

**Don’t buy corned beef.** Brisket that been preserved with salt and flavorings is [corned beef](https://amazingribs.com/tested-recipes/beef-and-bison-recipes/home-made-corned-beef-recipe/). It is not suitable for this recipe! To make Texas brisket, you need raw beef. That said, if you love corned beef (and we do!), check out our recipes showing you [how to make homemade corned beef](https://amazingribs.com/tested-recipes/beef-and-bison-recipes/home-made-corned-beef-recipe/) and how to enjoy it dishes like [Corned Beef and Cabbage](https://amazingribs.com/tested-recipes/beef-and-bison-recipes/traditional-corned-beef-and-cabbage-recipe/) and [Corned Beef Hash](https://amazingribs.com/tested-recipes/beef-and-bison-recipes/corned-beef-hash-recipe/).

## How to prep brisket for smoking

**Trim.** The thick fat cap does not penetrate the meat when it melts. I explain why in my article on [the myth of melting fat penetrating meat](file:///more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/melting-fat-cap-myth). But a little fat does help provide some moisture, plus most people will not cut off a thin fat cap at the table, so your flavorful BBQ rub remains and makes the brisket taste better.  I trim off most of the fat from the surface, leaving about a 1/4″ (6.3 mm). Some folks like to trim fat from between the flat and the point muscles too. Go for it, but don’t cut all the way through if you want the two muscles to remain attached. Render all your trimmed fat in a saucepan and discover the glories of [how to use beef tallow](https://amazingribs.com/how-to-render-animal-fats-for-cooking-the-easy-way/).

**Separate (optional).** In a Texas bar, this topic will usually end in gunplay. The truth is that you _can_ separate the muscles while trimming the brisket. Purists cry heresy, and I’m with them if this is your first rodeo. It’s just easier and more traditional to leave a whole packer brisket whole. However, separating makes a lot of sense. It makes the flat more uniform in thickness, so it cooks more evenly. Plus, you can apply your dry rub to all sides of the flat for more flavor, and you will get an all-around smoke ring. You can then cook the flat and point side by side and easily turn the point into that luscious beef candy known as [burnt ends](https://amazingribs.com/brisket-burnt-ends/). Separate and you also knock off about 1/3 of the cooking time compared to a whole packer. Let’s say you separate and remove most of the fat from a 13 pound (5.8 kg) packer. You can expect about 5 pounds (2.2 kg) of flat, 4 pounds (1.8 kg) of point, and 4 pounds (1.8 kg) of trim. The point end is usually a bit thinner, so if you separate, you might want to put the point on the smoker an hour or two after the flat.

**Inject (optional)**. If you have a [hypodermic syringe for injecting meat](file:///ratings-reviews/tools/injectors/), now’s the time to use it. Injecting is strictly optional but it does keep the meat juicy. Injecting with beef broth, [brine](file:///tested-recipes/salting-brining-curing-and-injecting/salting-and-wet-brining/), or a specially formulated injection helps combat dehydration, and the salt enhances flavor. Many of the competition champs have been injecting with a product called Fab B Light or [Butcher BBQ Brisket Marinade](https://butcherbbq.com/?pg=directionsforuse), both of which are moisturizers, tenderizers, and flavor enhancers. Traditionalists think this is way too Barry Bonds and are repulsed by the idea. The results speak for themselves. They are winning. A lot. I like to inject for moisture but I also like brisket to taste like beef, so I use plain beef broth. Just pump in about 1 ounce (28 g) of [beef broth](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-ingredients-glossaries/soup-stock-gravy-and-bouillon/) per pound of raw meat by inserting the needle parallel to the grain in several locations about 1″ (2.5 cm) apart and backing it out as you press the plunger. Do it in the sink, and be careful so you don’t get squirted in the eye.

**Season.** If you injected the meat with salted beef broth, then do not salt the meat. On the other hand, if you didn’t inject, then salt the meat about 12 to 24 hours in advance so it can work its way in. Worse case, salt it at least 2 to 4 hours before cooking. Sprinkle on about 1/2 teaspoon per pound of meat evenly over the meat surface. Both injecting and salting harness [the benefits of brining meat](https://amazingribs.com/technique-and-science/salting-brining-curing-and-injecting/): enhanced juiciness and flavor. Some cooks mark the meat grain direction now with a slice in the surface, or they cut off a slice following the grain. That’s so they can properly slice the cooked meat across the grain later.

**Rub.** After salting, sprinkle my [Big Bad Beef Rub](file:///tested-recipes/spice-rubs-and-pastes/big-bad-beef-rub-recipe/) liberally on all areas of the meat and rub it in evenly. This rub is perfect for BBQ brisket, as it helps darken and flavor the [brisket bark](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/what-is-bark/). Want to go strictly traditional? Use “dalmation rub,” just salt and pepper. That means only adding pepper after brining with an injection or with salt. Note: You _can_ put the salt and spice rub on at the same time, but the rub flavors won’t penetrate the meat, so I put mine on while the smoker is heating up, about an hour before cooking. This approach is discussed in my article on the [Science of Rubs](file:///tested-recipes/spice-rubs-and-pastes/science-of-rubs/). Some cooks like to help the rub stick to the meat by slathering on mustard or beef tallow first. Our President, [Clint Cantwell](https://amazingribs.com/about-us/meet-our-team/clint-cantwell/), uses mayonnaise because, as he says “mayo is fat, and fat is flavor!” Don’t get too bogged down in slathers. My experience is that they make little or no difference in the final outcome so I tend to skip them. 

**Chill.** Keep the meat chilled until just before you cook it. [Chilled meat attracts more smoke through a process called thermophoresis](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/grill-and-smoker-setup-and-firing/science-of-wood-and-smoke/).

## How to wrap brisket

![wrapped brisket on smoker for barbecue brisket recipe.](https://amazingribs.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/brisket-on-smoker-1024x768.jpg)

Big, dense clods of meat like beef brisket and pork shoulder tend to hit a point during smoking when the internal temperature stops rising. Sometimes for hours. This maddening phenomenon is known as the [BBQ stall](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/understanding-and-beating-barbecue-stall/). But there’s a way to beat it: [the Texas Crutch](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/texas-crutch/). Just wrap the hunk of meat in foil or butcher paper. The meat braises and steams in its own juices, powering through the stall. It does soften the bark a bit, but it makes the meat more tender and juicy. Purists say wrapping is not _traditional._ Spare me. Cooking with charcoal in a steel tube is not traditional either. You want tradition? Go dig a pit in the dirt. I always crutch brisket, and here’s how.

**Wait for the stall.** At around 150 to 165°F internal temp (after 6 to 8 hours of smoking), the internal temperature will stop rising steadily. That’s when to wrap.

**Use foil or butcher paper.** Foil makes moister meat with a slight pot roast texture and a soft bark, but it can cut 2 hours off the cooking time. Butcher paper cuts about 1 hour off cooking time, lets some moisture escape, and tastes very much like the foil wrapped version. Both materials trap some rendered fat and juices, so the differences are subtle. [Austin brisket master Aaron Franklin](https://franklinbbq.com/) uses butcher paper, and you can [buy pink butcher paper online here](https://amzn.to/2bf1TU5). 

**Crimp it tight.** Place the brisket on a long sheet of double-layered heavy-duty foil or on butcher paper. Drizzle on 1/2 cup beef broth (some folks added a little beef tallow as well), and then [wrap the brisket](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/texas-crutch/), crimping and folding the foil or paper tightly around the meat as you go down the sheet of foil or paper.

**Keep cooking.** Continue cooking the wrapped brisket at 225°F either on the smoker or in an oven (it won’t pick up any more smoke when wrapped). Finishing in the oven is cheating, but nobody will arrest you, and it might be your only option if you’re having trouble controlling the temperature of your outdoor cooker. What about the bark? If you have a good bark when you wrap, most of it will survive. It will soften some, so if you prefer it really crusty, crisp up the bark over high heat when you unwrap.

## How to make burnt ends

![Sauced smoked brisket burnt ends in an aluminum pan.](https://amazingribs.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/burnt-ends-1024x461.jpg)

Burnt ends are amazingly flavorful bite-size crispy meat cubes. Originally they were simply edges and ends that were overcooked and trimmed off and munched by the kitchen staff. If there were any leftover, they were given away for free.

Then, in 1970, in his marvelous book American Fried, Calvin Trillin wrote the following about Arthur Bryant’s restaurant in Kansas City “The main course at Bryant’s, as far as I’m concerned, is something that is given away for free — the burned edges of the brisket. The counterman just pushes them over to the side as he slices the beef, and anyone who wants them helps himself. I dream of those burned edges. Sometimes, when I’m in some awful overpriced restaurant in some strange town — all of my restaurant-finding techniques having failed, so that I’m left to choke down something that costs seven dollars and tastes like a medium-rare sponge — a blank look comes over my face: I have just realized that at that very moment someone in Kansas City is being given those burned edges free.”

To make burnt ends, cut the cooked point muscle into bite-size cubes, about 1/2″ to 3/4″ (12 to 19 mm). Set aside any pieces that are too fatty or just eat them. And here’s my technique, strictly illegal in BBQ competitions, but very welcome in my family. In a frying pan or even in a disposable pan on the grill, render about 1/4 pound of the beef fat that you trimmed from the brisket. Or use bacon fat or duck fat. Put the cubes in the pan and gently fry the cubes until they are crunchy on the outside, turning them a few times. Drain the fat and add about 1/4 cup (60 ml) of your favorite BBQ sauce and 1/4 cup (60 ml) of the drippings from the foil used for [the Texas Crutch](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/texas-crutch/). You can do all this outside. Put the pan back over the heat and close the lid of your cooker (or add a lid if you’re indoors at this point). Stir every 5 minutes or so. Let the cubes absorb most of the liquid and start to sizzle, but don’t let them burn. When they’re done, keep them warm in a [faux cambro](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/faux-cambro/) with the cooked flat muscle.

## The best smoked brisket recipe, step by step

New to brisket? Follow the simple path first: start with a whole packer brisket (Choice grade or better), trim the fat cap to 1/4 inch, dry brine, smoke at 225°F, wrap when the brisket hits the stall and the bark is set, cook until the meat is wobbly and probe-tender, hold it wrapped for at least 2 hours, then slice it against the grain. 

Advanced cooks may want to take other paths like separating the point muscle from the flat muscle, injecting the meat with broth, making burnt ends, and slicing the finished brisket like they do in BBQ competitions. My advice: start simple before you go playing around. Your guests will thank you.

## Smoked brisket FAQs

**What temperature should you smoke brisket at?** 

I like 225°F for this recipe. Many competitors agree that low and slow, around 225°F (107°C) for 16 to 20 hours for a whole packer brisket, is necessary to make the meat tender and juicy. However, legendary “Barbecue King” [Walter Jetton](file:///barbecue-history-and-culture/barbecue-diplomacy-lbjs-texas-white-house/), Lyndon Johnson’s caterer, advocated cooking brisket at 275°F (135°C) and up. [John Fullilove](https://www.texasmonthly.com/bbq/where-is-smittys-pitmaster-john-fullilove/) at the Texas brisket mecca [Smitty’s Market](http://smittysmarket.com/) confesses that he cranks the heat over 300°F (149°C) and knocks out his briskets in as little as 8 hours. I have seen competitors take home big prize checks with brisket cooked at 350°F (167°C). And chef-scientist Chris Young swears by his [6-Hour Brisket (Hot and Fast Brisket Recipe)](https://amazingribs.com/hot-and-fast-brisket/) that he generously shared on our website. The bottom line is that cooking temp seems to be less important than other factors. But because it is difficult to make brisket tender, I advocate for low and slow until you have mastered the techniques and are certain that your meat source and methods are superior.

**Do you need to mop brisket as it cooks?**

I skip it because it softens the bark, cools the meat and slows cooking. I prefer to inject moisture _before_ cooking instead. That said, the AmazingRibs.com science advisor, [Prof. Greg Blonder](file:///about-us/meet-our-team/prof-greg-blonder/) has proven that wet meat holds more smoke, so mopping or spritzing with water, beef broth, or apple juice will yield a smokier brisket. Will flavored liquids help? No. There just aren’t enough taste molecules in apple juice or beer to change the taste of brisket. Not like a sprinkle of a spice. For more on the subject, read my article on [the science of basting](file:///more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/mythbusting-basting-mopping-and-spritzing/).

**How do you tell when brisket is done?** 

Use a good digital thermometer to monitor your cooker and another to monitor the meat. Your smoker’s dial thermometer is wrong. Don’t trust it. For the meat, the [Combustion Predictive Thermometer](https://amazingribs.com/ratings-reviews/thermometers/combustion-predictive-thermometer-review/) is perfect for the job. Step into the digital age. As a rule of thumb, aim for a finished meat temperature of between 195°F and 205°F, though seasoned professionals go by feel versus temperature as each brisket cooks differently. Poke it with a finger or pick it up and jiggle it. If it goes “wubba wubba” and wiggles like jelly, you know the connective tissues have melted and it is done. You can also poke it with a thermometer probe. It should slide in and out with only a little resistance.

**How do you cook brisket ahead of time?**

My answer is “don’t do it”. That’s called serving leftovers. Brisket is best fresh off the smoker or out of the [faux cambro](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/faux-cambro/). If you have to serve it at noon on Sunday, then start cooking before you go to bed on Saturday. Now if you absolutely, positively must finish cooking it Saturday and then serve it Sunday, here’s [a different brisket cooking and holding technique](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/cook-today-serve-tomorrow-wozniak-way/) used by award-winning pitmaster Mike Wozniak.

**Does brisket need barbecue sauce?**

To some people, it’s not barbecue unless it has a sweet red sauce on it. Not to Texans. “In Texas, we celebrate great brisket by not messing with it,” says Daniel Vaughn, the barbecue critic for [Texas Monthly](https://www.texasmonthly.com/food/the-list-the-top-50-barbecue-joints-in-texas/). “If it’s done right, then you slice it pencil thick and slap it on a piece of butcher paper. It’s naked, quivering and vulnerable, so it has to stand on its own.” In some places, a thin decidedly not-sweet, tomato-y gravy-like _[jus](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/ingredients-glossaries/cooking-and-barbecue-lingo/#jus)_ is tolerated.

## BBQ brisket timeline: how long does smoked brisket take?

Below is a timetable for the average brisket cook. Plan on 14 to 18 hours for a whole packer brisket cooked at 225°F and wrapped in foil when the meat hits 150°F (65°C). That includes about two hours holding time. If you wrap in butcher paper instead of foil, add another one to two hours cooking time. If you don’t wrap in foil or paper, plan on 16 to 20 hours cooking plus two hours holding. For brisket flats, plan on 10 to 12 hours with foil, 12 to 14 hours without foil. The meat thickness, the cooker temperature and whether you wrap or not are the most important determinants of total cooking time for BBQ brisket. But humidity, ambient air temp, wind, rain, and grade of meat can all play a role. ## Pitmaster Deep Dive: What to do with brisket leftovers

If you have leftovers that you will not be able to scarf down in a few days, mix them with a bit of barbecue sauce or the jus from the Texas Crutch (if it is not too salty), and freeze everything in zipper bags or vacuum bags. The sauce helps prevents freezer burn. Pop a bag in the microwave and you’ve got a great emergency meal for two. Second best is to heat leftovers slowly in a pot with the lid on. Here are some other ways to transform brisket into a new dish.

-   **Shepherd’s Pie and Cottage Pie.** Probably the best thing I’ve ever done with leftover meat from my smoked brisket recipe is a variation on Shepherd’s Pie or Cottage Pie, which is essentially meat and [mashed potatoes](https://amazingribs.com/tested-recipes/potato-recipes/garlic-mashed-potatoes-recipe/) in two layers. Brown some carrots, celery, onion, and frozen peas in a cast iron pan, then toss in cubes of leftover brisket with some of the _jus_ and/or some beef stock. Top them with about 2″ (5 cm) of mashed spuds, paint the top with melted butter, sprinkle on some [parmesan cheese](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-ingredients-glossaries/parmigiano-reggiano/) and bread crumbs, and bake the whole shootin’ match in a 350°F oven until the top is brown. OMG. Substitute BBQ lamb and you’ve got Shepherd’s Pie.
-   **Brisket enchiladas**. [Slow’s Bar-B-Q](https://slowsbarbq.com/) in Detroit is famous for this dish. They sauté onions, toss in sliced brisket and a splash of hot sauce, Worcestershire sauce, and their secret house sauce. -   **Hash.** John R. Crowley from Denver (a member of [our Pitmaster Club, where many brilliant BBQ ideas are shared](https://amazingribs.com/information-about-our-pitmaster-club/)) says he likes to chop up leftovers, mix them in beans, or fry them up in some hash.
-   **On scrambled eggs or salads.** Bill Martin likes his leftovers chopped up in scrambled eggs and on top of a salad.

## Authentic Texas-Style Smoked BBQ Brisket

[Rate this Recipe](#commentform)

If you love BBQ beef brisket but have always been intimidated by the thought of smoking one yourself at home then this is the perfect "how to" smoked brisket recipe to help you get it done to perfection.

Prep Time 10 minutes

Cook Time 12 hours

Total Time 12 hours 10 minutes

Servings: 12 (approximately 1 pound (454 g) starting weight per person)

Course: Dinner, Main Course

### Special Tools

-    4 cups wood chunks or chips (about 8 ounces (226 g)) 
    

### Method 

1.  **Trim.** Trim off most of the fat cap but leave about 1/4" (6.3 mm). Clean the meaty side of any silverskin, a shiny, thin, tough membrane. Set aside some fat for making burnt ends if you're going that route. I always freeze some of the fat and grind it for my [burgers](https://amazingribs.com/tested-recipes/hamburger-sloppy-joe-salisbury-steak-recipes/grinding-your-own-hamburgers/). I also [render beef fat in a saucepan](https://amazingribs.com/how-to-render-animal-fats-for-cooking-the-easy-way/), and freeze that too. I use beef fat to [paint my steaks with fat just before searing](https://amazingribs.com/tested-recipes/beef-and-bison-recipes/grill-better-steakhouse-steaks/). If you want to separate the point and the flat muscles before cooking, now's the time to do it (see Notes).
    

4.  **Cook.** Put the meat on the cooker. I strongly recommend you use a [remote digital thermometer](https://amazingribs.com/) and insert the probe with the tip centered in the thickest part of the meat furthest from the heat. On a smoker with a water pan, put the meat right above the water. Place the oven temp probe on the grate next to the meat. Add about 2 cups (4 ounces (113 g) of [smoking wood](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/grill-and-smoker-setup-and-firing/science-of-wood-and-smoke/) right after the meat goes on. When the smoke stops, add 4 ounces more during the first 2 hours, which usually means adding some every 30 minutes or so. Keep an eye on the water in the pan. Don't let it dry out. After 3 hours, turn the meat over if the color is different from top to bottom. Otherwise, leave the meat alone.
    
5.  **Wrap.** [The Texas Crutch](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/texas-crutch-wrap-foil-tenderize-and-speed-cooking/) is optional but I always crutch brisket. It helps beat  [the BBQ stall.](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/understanding-and-beating-barbecue-stall/) When the [brisket bark](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/what-is-bark/) is set and the internal temp stops rising, at an internal temp of about 150°F to 165°F (after about 6 to 8 hours), put the brisket on a long sheet of double-layered heavy-duty foil or butcher paper, drizzle on 1/2 cup beef broth, and [wrap the brisket](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/texas-crutch/), crimping and folding tightly around the meat. Then continue cooking it wrapped at 225°F (either on the smoker or in an oven).
    
6.  **Hold it.** When the meat temp hits 195°F (95°C), after another 5 to 7 hours, start poking it. When it is done, a thermometer probe will slide in and out with little resistance. Poke it with a finger or pick it up and jiggle it. If it goes "wubba wubba" and wiggles like jelly, it is done. This usually happens at around 203°F (95°C). At that point, [hold the wrapped brisket in a faux cambro](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/faux-cambro/) [(plastic cooler)](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/faux-cambro/) for 2 to 4 hours. That extra time helps tenderize the meat by allowing some [carryover cooking](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/carryover-cooking/) to soften tough connective tissue. And it’s a great fudge factor that lets you take the meat off when it is ready and hold it for a few hours until the guests are ready.
    
7.  **Slice.** Wait to slice until the last possible minute. Brisket dries out very quickly when cut. Turn the meat _fat side up_ so the juices will run onto the meat as you slice. The easy way is to lop off about 1" (2 cm) from the thick end and about 2" (5 cm) from the thin end, which is the tip of the flat. These ends are likely overcooked and dry. Chop them and smother them in sauce for chopped brisket. Then find the fat layer between the point and flat and slide your blade between the two muscles. Separate them, and trim off most of the excess fat. Find the grain of the flat and cut across the grain into slices 1/8″ to 1/4″ (3 to 6 mm) thick, or about as thick as a pencil. You can also slice against the grain of the point, and offer your guests "lean" (flat) or "fatty" (point). Most will choose the lean, which will leave the better, fattier, point cut for you. Turn it into burnt ends!
    
8.  **Serve.** If the brisket is perfectly cooked, it should be moist and juicy. You can serve it simply sliced on a plate or as a sandwich made with Texas Toast. If you want to drizzle on the juices, taste them first: they can be salty. In that case, you can dilute them with water or unsalted beef broth. You can also mix them with some [Texas Barbecue Mop Sauce](https://amazingribs.com/tested-recipes/barbecue-sauce-recipes/texas-barbecue-juice/).
    

### Notes

_**About separating.** The simple, traditional path is to smoke the whole packer brisket. But the point muscle and the flat muscle will cook more evenly if you separate them. Just slide your knife into the fat layer between the two muscles, separate them, and trim off excess fat. You can cook the point and flat side by side, and then turn the point into burnt ends._ 

_**About injecting.** This step is optional but it makes the meat juicier. Use a [hypodermic syringe for injecting meat](https://amazingribs.com/ratings-reviews/tools/injectors/) to pump in about 1 ounce (28 g) of [beef broth](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-ingredients-glossaries/soup-stock-gravy-and-bouillon/#broth) per pound of raw meat (about 12 ounces, or 1 1/2 cups, for a 12-pound trimmed whole packer brisket. Insert the needle parallel to the grain of the meat in several locations about 1″ (2.5 cm) apart and backing it out as you press the plunger. Do it in the sink. And if you inject with beef broth, do not salt the meat, as the sodium in the broth takes the place of the exterior salt._

**_About the salt._** _Remember, kosher salt is half the concentration of table salt so if you use table salt, use half as much. Click here to read more [about salt and how it works.](https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-ingredients-glossaries/science-of-salt/)_

_**About the rub.** If you’d rather not make our [Big Bad Beef Rub](https://amazingribs.com/tested-recipes/spice-rubs-and-pastes/big-bad-beef-rub-recipe/) from scratch you can buy it instead. We have created a bottled rub that is very similar and you can [buy our bottled brisket rub here](https://amazingribs.com/flavor/#red). Keep in mind that our bottled rubs have salt in them. When using a bottled rub here, do not add the salt called for in the recipe. Just use the rub instead, and add it when the salt is called for._

_**About crisping the bark.** If you wrapped the brisket and you want the bark crispier, firm it up by unwrapping the meat and putting it over a hot grill or under a broiler for a few minutes on each side. Watch it closely so it doesn’t burn._

_**Sauce is optional.** You can serve the sliced brisket with about 2 cups [Texas Barbecue Mop Sauce](https://amazingribs.com/tested-recipes/barbecue-sauce-recipes/texas-barbecue-juice/ "Texas Barbecue Juice") (you can make this days in advance). Mix in some of the brisket drippings if you like. Hell, you can eat it however you like too. At Joe’s KC in Kansas City they serve a sandwich called the “Z-Man”. It’s thin sliced brisket with a sweet KC sauce topped with melted provolone cheese, a couple of thick crunchy onion rings, more sauce, all on a toasted kaiser roll, and slaw on the side. You can get pickle chips on it too._

## Pitmaster Deep Dive: Brisket anatomy

![Anatomy illustration of a full packer brisket showing the point muscle, flat muscle, fat cap, and fat layers.](https://amazingribs.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/brisket.jpg)

Briskets are the pectoral muscles from the chest of the steer between the forelegs. Each animal has two, and because cattle have no collarbones, these boneless muscles bear quite a load. There isn’t much fat marbling within the muscle, and there’s a lot of springy connective tissue in and around the muscle fibers. That’s why briskets are so tough.

Much of the world’s brisket is made into corned beef, pastrami, or pot roast, but it is also a fine cut for barbecue, and it is required in cooking contests sanctioned by the [Kansas City Barbecue Society](https://kcbs.us/) (KCBS), about 500 across the nation.

### The two muscles on a brisket

There are two distinct muscles in a brisket that comes from the meat packer whole: a long, flat, rectangular, lean muscle that sometimes comes to a point that is called the flat (_pectoralis profundus_), and a narrower, thicker, fattier, oval shaped muscle called the point (_pectoralis superficialis_). Got it? The flat is pointy and the point is oval. Go figure.

When you buy a whole packer brisket, it usually weighs 8 to 16 pounds (3.6 to 7.2 kg) and comes vacuum packed in an airtight plastic bag. The meat has a cap of fat on one side that can be up to 1″ (2.54cm) thick, and it is trimmed pretty close to fat free on the other side. There is a thick layer of fat that separates the point from the flat.

### Cooking a hunk o’ flat or a hunk o’ point

A whole packer brisket is a lot of meat! Many grocers cut up the whole brisket into smaller more manageable sizes. I often see cuts from the flat (a.k.a. “first cut”) or point (“second cut” or “deckle”) running anywhere from three to six pounds. Flats seem to be more common than points. If the meat case has both, choose the point. It has more marbling in the muscle and will be more tender, flavorful, and juicy. If you see only flats, ask the butcher if you can order points.

Either way, the best briskets are wet aged 28 to 45 days in a vacuum bag. Enzymes within muscles tenderize meat as it ages, so competitors often wet age their meat in the vacuum bag in the fridge in their basement. Click here for [more about aging beef](https://amazingribs.com/technique-and-science/cooking-science/aging-beef-at-home/).

## Pitmaster Deep Dive: Left-handed briskets?

_Tom Hoefer from Allen, Texas, posted this tall tale about a barbecue contest on the net in 2001. It is reprinted here, slightly edited, with his permission. Fact or fiction? Serious or joke? You decide…_

A few years back at the Texas State Finals, several of us arrived on Thursday to get in line for the best sites. Thursday night was devoted to serious drinking.

One of the better cooks, Ole Connie Baker of the team “Li’l Pit Of Heaven” was throwing back quite a few of those Mexican beers with a chunk of lime stuck in the neck. Connie had so many of them limeade beers that he was starting to smile with a pucker.

Someone asked him how come his brisket was so tender and always placed in the top three. I thought to myself, boy oh boy, if loose lips sink ships then Ole Connie is going down tonight. All got quiet as he stuffed another lime in a longneck and he said that he “only cooks left-handed briskets”.

He explained that most, but not all, steers rest on their left side, which means when they get up they have to push harder with their right legs. At this point about half the bunch mumbled something to the effect of “bull hockey” and went back to different conversations.

A few of us noticed that Ole Connie wasn’t smirking. Two or three of us moved closer and I said to him, “You can’t stop there. What does pushing up with their right legs have to do with the left brisket?”

Ole Connie stuffed another lime and told us that when they push up with the right legs, it flexes the right brisket muscle more than the left. Therefore the right-handed brisket will be tougher and less marbled than the left. Not always but usually. I asked him, “How the heck do you tell a left-handed brisket from the right?”

He stuffed another lime and told me that, with the fat side down, on a left-handed brisket, with the narrow part closest to you, the point will curve to the right.

Saturday awards time rolled around and Connie took First Brisket and Grand Champion over 180 of the best cooks in Texas. I think that I came in 19th with my right-handed brisket.

This is a concept that I just could not get this off my mind. Then I phoned the kin folk in LaGrange, Texas, and asked if they would check out their herd. Yep, you guessed it. Only three out of 37 consistently rested on their right side. Dangnation, Ole Connie has got it going big time!

I went to five different grocery stores and flexed briskets to see which sides were more limber and more marbled. There are some right-handed briskets that are more limber and marbled than the lefties, but for the most part, the majority of the best are left handed!

Welp, there it is folks. Take it or leave it. As Joe Friday on the 1950s TV show, Dragnet, used to say, “Only the facts, ma’am.”

## Pitmaster Deep Dive: Brisket slicing methods

Brisket dries out quickly when cut, so wait until the last possible minute to slice. Then cut across the grain into slices 1/8″ to 1/4″ (3 to 6 mm) thick, or about as thick as a pencil. We slice across the grain to make the meat easier to chew (more tender). But slicing can be tricky because there are two muscles (the point and the flat) and the grain flows in different directions.

### The classic brisket slicing method

![Smoked brisket diagram for slicing](https://amazingribs.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/brisket-gas-setup.jpg)

In this photo you can see the grain in the meat. The point muscle sits on top of the flat muscle. The point is thin on one end (A) and thick on the other (B). The easy slicing method is to first cut off an inch or two from both ends because these ends are most likely overcooked and dry; chop them up and douse them with [Texas Mop sauce](https://amazingribs.com/tested-recipes/barbecue-sauce-recipes/texas-barbecue-juice/). Then find the fat layer between the point and flat and slide your knife between the two muscles. Separate them, and trim off most of the excess fat. Find the grain of the flat and cut across the grain into slices 1/8″ to 1/4″ (3 to 6 mm) thick, or about as thick as a pencil. Do the same with the point. 

### The competition brisket slicing method

![brisket in lettuce box](https://amazingribs.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/brisket-box-1.jpg)

Some competitors prefer this method. As in the classic method, you run your knife between the flat and the point to separate the two muscles, trim off excess fat, and slice the flat across the grain about 1/4″ (6.3 mm) thick. The meat should hold together, not fall apart or crumble. It should only pull apart with a gentle tug. If the first slice falls apart, cut thicker slices. The difference here is that the point is turned into chunks of [burnt ends](https://amazingribs.com/brisket-burnt-ends/) and served with nice, even slices of the flat. The image above shows a competition brisket entry by [Kansas City Barbecue Society (KCBS)](https://www.kcbs.us/) President Emeritus Candy Weaver. Click here to learn more about [how to cook competition brisket](file:///tested-recipes/beef-and-bison-recipes/award-winning-competition-brisket-recipe).

### The Sorkin brisket slicing method

I learned this method from Barry Sorkin of Chicago’s [Smoque BBQ](http://smoquebbq.com/). He makes my favorite brisket _in the world._ In the photos below, Sorkin demonstrates how he slices a whole packer brisket.

![Slicing brisket and removing the thin part of the flat.](https://amazingribs.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/removing-brisket-flat.jpg)

1\. Start by removing the drier thin part of the flat, and set it aside for chopping, not slicing.

![Slicing a brisket in the thick center part of the flat.](https://amazingribs.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/slicing-brisket1.jpg)

2\. Then slice the thick center part of the flat across the grain until you encounter the point muscle on top of the flat. In the photo above, he is within one or two slices from hitting the point. These center cut slices are the ones that most competitors use because they produce a visually pleasing presentation of nearly identical slabs of meat.

![Barry Sorkin removing fat from a cooked brisket.](https://amazingribs.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/removing-fat.jpg)

3\. Sorkin then goes into the layer of fat between the point and flat at the thick butt end, and he removes much of the fat. It can be 1/2″ (13 mm) thick or more in there, and that makes the slices inedible.

![Barry Sorkin cutting a brisket in half through the point and the flat muscles.](https://amazingribs.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/splitting-point.jpg)

4\. The remaining hunk has both muscles, the point sitting on top of the flat, with the grain going in different directions. Sorkin cuts this hunk in half.

![BBQ brisket cut in half during the Barry Sorkin brisket slicing method.](https://amazingribs.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/slicing-brisket2.jpg)

5\. Here, the piece on the right is a butt end with one cut edge. The piece on the left, from the center of the brisket, has two cut edges.

![Slicing a brisket](https://amazingribs.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/slicing-point.jpg)

6\. Now, slice the center piece as shown, from the outer edge in. You’re slicing perpendicular to the two cut edges shown in the previous image.

![Barry Sorkin slicing a smoked brisket.](https://amazingribs.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/slicing-brisket4.jpg)

7\. Slice the other piece, the butt end of the point, in the same direction as it was originally cut, continuing to slice parallel to the cut end.

![Sliced brisket sandwich](https://amazingribs.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/brisket-sandwich2.jpg)

8\. Sorkin then fans the slices on a bun (those are slices not a whole piece). Notice the line separating flat and point. And, yes, this is a brioche bun. That’s how Sorkin likes to serve it.

![Chopping brisket](https://amazingribs.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/chopped-brisket.jpg)

9\. Finally, Sorkin chops the thin parts of the flat along with some of the fat from between the point and flat mixed in for moisture. The results are crunchy, heavily seasoned, and juicy. These bits can also be splashed with sauce and served on a bun.
